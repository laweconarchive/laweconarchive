# Literature Review: Recent Law and Economics Research

**Date:** 2026-08-17
**Query:** New or materially revised law-and-economics research released after Issue 003
**Review window:** 2026-08-11 through 2026-08-17

## Summary

Six CEPR Discussion Papers in the review window meet the weekly report's scope. They study creditor-rights design, delegation inside bank supervision, the electoral consequences of offshoring, common ownership and explicit collusion, institutional explanations for the size premium, and the expanding sovereign-bank-NBFI risk nexus.

The issue contains empirical institutional analysis, organizational modeling paired with a policy reform, linked political and industry data, ownership-cartel matching, meta-analysis, and multi-level financial exposure analysis. Every bibliographic fact and result summary was checked against the official CEPR paper page.

## Key Papers

### Kornejew, Kundu, and Müller (2026) — Credit Cycles and Creditor Rights

- **Main contribution:** Relates creditor protection to the severity of post-boom losses and the reallocation of credit.
- **Method:** Panel of 39 countries from 1978–2019 plus firm-level evidence around Delaware antirecharacterization laws.
- **Key finding:** Stronger protection is followed by smaller output losses, fewer non-performing loans, and greater reallocation away from unproductive firms.
- **Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp21848

### Dessein, Gong, Lambert, and Wagner (2026) — Subordinates in Charge

- **Main contribution:** Studies whether delegation reduces information loss inside bank-supervision hierarchies.
- **Method:** Organizational model plus a reform delegating decisions over a subset of bank branches.
- **Key finding:** Affected branches become 57–80% more likely to face intervention; reported tests are inconsistent with greater general stringency or greater bank risk-taking.
- **Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp21846

### Mayda, Oldenski, and Steingress (2026) — Offshoring and Voting Patterns in the US

- **Main contribution:** Connects heterogeneous labor-market exposure to incumbent voting.
- **Method:** County voting and industry offshoring data from 2000–2020.
- **Key finding:** Incumbent vote shares rise in more college-educated counties and fall in counties more exposed through manufacturing and offshorable occupations.
- **Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp21844

### Abraham, Ederer, and Marvão (2026) — Common Ownership and Collusion

- **Main contribution:** Provides evidence on common ownership and explicit cartel participation without relying only on detected illegal cartels.
- **Method:** Hand-collected ownership data linked to the universe of registered Swedish legal cartels.
- **Key finding:** Higher firm-pair profit weights predict participation and longer cartel duration, with an asymmetric relation between the internalizing firm and its rival.
- **Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp21841

### Schwarz, Havranek, Irsova, and Novak (2026) — Trust, Rule of Law, and the Size Premium

- **Main contribution:** Organizes cross-study disagreement about the small-firm return premium using formal and informal institutions.
- **Method:** Meta-analysis of 1,613 estimates from 105 studies and 31 countries, including Bayesian model averaging.
- **Key finding:** Rule of law has the more stable association and corresponds to larger conventional size premia; trust is conditional and less precise.
- **Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp21840

### Avdjiev, Hardy, and Jager (2026) — The Evolving Nexus

- **Main contribution:** Extends analysis of the sovereign-bank nexus to non-bank financial institutions.
- **Method:** European bank-level and global country-level exposure and risk data.
- **Key finding:** Direct sovereign exposure has become less important for bank-sovereign co-movement, while bank-NBFI exposure and NBFI sovereign holdings have become more important.
- **Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp21839

## Thematic Organization

### Theoretical Contributions

The bank-supervision paper models how hierarchy creates bias and information loss. The remaining papers mainly use empirical evidence to discipline institutional mechanisms involving creditor protection, ownership incentives, voting, legal institutions, and balance-sheet networks.

### Empirical Findings

Creditor rights may improve reallocation after credit booms; lower-level delegation may improve supervisory intervention; common ownership is associated with explicit collusion; and non-bank balance sheets increasingly form part of sovereign-financial risk transmission. Offshoring and the size premium show strong heterogeneity across local workforces and institutional environments.

### Methodological Innovations

The issue's most distinctive designs are the pairing of a 39-country panel with a Delaware legal reform, a complete legal-cartel registry matched to ownership, and Bayesian model averaging across a large meta-analytic estimate set.

## Gaps and Opportunities

1. Separate the causal effect of specific creditor-rights provisions from correlated financial institutions.
2. Test whether supervisory intervention improvements translate into final enforcement accuracy and deterrence.
3. Examine common ownership and collusion in contemporary settings where cartel formation is hidden.
4. Identify mechanisms behind the rule-of-law association with the size premium.
5. Evaluate which prudential and disclosure rules address NBFI-mediated sovereign risk without shifting risk elsewhere.

## Suggested Next Steps

- Monitor later working-paper versions and journal placements for design and result changes.
- Compare the creditor-rights findings with insolvency reforms outside Delaware.
- Track whether the bank-supervision and common-ownership papers release replication materials.
- Connect the sovereign-bank-NBFI results to concrete disclosure, liquidity, and capital-rule proposals.
